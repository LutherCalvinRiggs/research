# Technical Content Distribution: B2B Playbook

**Source:** The Builder's Guild (group chat screenshots, July 2026) — discussion participants: Jed Arden, Andrew Haim, and others
**Saved:** 2026-07-31
**Tags:** business, writing, career, technology, productivity

> **Context:** Group chat discussion about distributing a long-form technical guide (jedarden.com/guides/workflow/). The guide covers building applications with agent fleets — a niche technical topic targeting a sophisticated B2B audience. Advice from Andrew Haim on distribution strategy; observations from Jed Arden on what's working. Genericized per scope rules — advice applies to any technical founder distributing long-form content.

---

## TL;DR
Distribution playbook for a long-form technical guide targeting a sophisticated B2B audience (professionals where premium consulting is a small expense). The guide's author acknowledged the challenge: the feed is personalized, making it hard to know what top-performing content in a target audience looks like. The group's advice: multi-platform syndication for backlinks, video for reach, 15–30 second clips for additional distribution, per-platform content, Twitter threading with quote-tweet strategies, and enabling comments despite spam risk. The content loop can run on autopilot once codified.

---

## The Core Distribution Strategy (Andrew Haim)

**For SEO and authority (Substack, LinkedIn, Medium):**
Post the same content on Substack, LinkedIn, and Medium to push authoritative backlinks to the original articles. These platforms have domain authority that amplifies the original source's search ranking.

**For brand ownership and traffic (video):**
1. Create a 60–90 minute script based on a few creators you like, or your own content, so it feels authentic
2. Record yourself (phone is fine)
3. Cut it into 15–30 second clips for additional distribution on a "clips" variation of the main channel
4. Always link back to main channel/video
5. Each video and clip gets relevant additional per-platform content (captions, hashtags, titles)

**Enable comments:** "Great stuff, it deserves to reach more people" — comments enable algorithmic distribution. The concern about spam ("bigger lift with accounts and spam protection, but largely solved problem that allows folks to participate") is real but manageable.

## The Deconstruction Method for Target Audience Research

> "Treat it like anything else — find all top-ranking content that's being served to you and deconstruct it."
> — Andrew Haim

**The problem:** Social feeds are personalized to the author, not to the audience they want to attract. A technical founder building in public sees content served to technical founders — not necessarily content that surfaces to PE firms, hedge funds, or other B2B buyers.

**The implication:** You cannot easily observe what top-performing content looks like from your target audience's perspective. Workarounds:
- Use a secondary account that follows the kind of content your target audience follows
- Study competitors or peers who are already visible to that audience
- Look at content that gets shared INTO your target audience's communities, not just what originates there

## Twitter/X-Specific Notes

Twitter has its own formula for viral distribution. One pattern that works (noted somewhat skeptically by the group): reposting your own thread with engagement framing — e.g., referring to the original post/author in third person ("this guy is literally giving away the winning formula for X"). Acknowledged as "so dumb" by the group but noted as effective.

The broader pattern: the quote-tweet and self-repost mechanism can amplify reach if done with authentic framing rather than obviously promotional language.

For technical content: thread format can work well for distilling long-form guides into consumable Twitter threads with a link to the full piece.

## The "Content Loop on Autopilot" Observation

One participant shared analytics showing meaningful growth (1.48K active users, 857 clicks, 81.4K impressions over 3 months) from a content approach they described as running "on autopilot." The observation:

> "I should take the time to codify the content loop that's allowing this to happen on autopilot."

The counter-observation on iteration:

> "I'm sure it can be better, but it's one of 50 things I'm needing done when it comes to running the company, so I'll settle for 'up and to the right.'"

**The practical principle:** A content distribution system that works well enough on autopilot is worth more than a perfect system you haven't built. Codify what's working, then iterate — don't chase optimization at the expense of shipping.

## The B2B Positioning Challenge

When targeting high-value buyers (where premium consulting is a small line item), standard content distribution tactics face friction:

- **Feed personalization** — you see content for people like you, not for your target buyers
- **BD angle is rough** — organic content-to-client conversion is harder to engineer than direct outreach
- **Platform mismatch** — the platforms where technical content performs well aren't always the platforms where institutional buyers spend time

The group didn't resolve this tension definitively. The implication: for sophisticated B2B audiences, content builds credibility and inbound interest but probably isn't the primary BD mechanism. It works in parallel with direct outreach, referrals, and network effects rather than replacing them.

## The Distribution Checklist (from the conversation)

For a long-form technical guide:

- [ ] Post to Substack, LinkedIn, and Medium (backlinks + authority)
- [ ] Enable comments
- [ ] Create 60–90 min video script, record with phone
- [ ] Cut into 15–30 second clips for clips channel
- [ ] Each video/clip gets per-platform captions, hashtags, titles
- [ ] Clips link back to main video and main article
- [ ] Thread version for Twitter/X
- [ ] Quote-tweet strategy for re-amplification
- [ ] Identify the content loop that's working and codify it before optimizing
